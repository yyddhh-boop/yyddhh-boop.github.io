Mathematics as Stabilized Information

Human Practice, Precision, Education, and Artificial Intelligence

Guiding question. How do physical systems - human brains, communities, written media, and artificial intelligence - acquire, stabilize, communicate, and use mathematical concepts?

Central wager. Mathematics should first be studied as a real activity within civilization. Its exceptional exactness does not separate it from information technology. Rather, mathematics may be one of the most demanding forms of information stabilization that human societies have built.

This essay grew out of a discussion recorded on August 29, 2026. It presents a developing research program, not a set of settled conclusions.

1. Begin with mathematical activity

A familiar philosophy of mathematics begins by asking whether numbers, sets, or geometric objects exist independently of us. That question may eventually matter, but I do not want to begin there. It starts too far away from the phenomenon I first want to understand.

Mathematics appears in history as something that people do. Mathematicians live in societies. They speak languages, draw diagrams, use instruments, write books, teach students, build institutions, and inherit standards from earlier communities. Mathematical practice has always been entangled with philosophy, astronomy, navigation, construction, trade, physics, engineering, and computation. The modern division of these activities into separate disciplines should not be projected backward as though it were fundamental.

My starting thesis is therefore methodological:

Before asking what mathematical objects are in themselves, study how mathematical practices arise, become reliable, spread through a community, and acquire the power to describe or control parts of the world.

This is not a rejection of philosophy. It is an attempt to give philosophy a more concrete object of study: counting, measuring, drawing, proving, teaching, calculating, designing, predicting, and controlling. Mathematics is treated first as a phenomenon inside human civilization.

2. Information technology in the broadest sense

The phrase information technology usually suggests computers, digital storage, or Shannon's theory. I am using it in a broader and more primitive sense.

Operational definition. An information technology is any organized physical process by which a state can be prepared, made sufficiently stable or repeatable, detected by another system, and used to guide further activity.

Under this definition, paper is an information technology. So is a blackboard. Spoken language is one as well: a controlled sequence of sound waves carries a pattern from one nervous system to another. Writing converts fleeting speech or thought into a more durable physical configuration.

Consider the written word apple. Different people form its letters differently. No two handwritten instances need to be geometrically identical. Yet a linguistic community recognizes the pattern and the order of the letters. The code is stable not because every physical mark is identical, but because some variations are ignored while certain relations are preserved.

This is already a model of abstraction. A successful representational practice distinguishes relevant variation from irrelevant variation. It allows different physical realizations to count as instances of the same usable pattern.

Biological perception belongs to the same story. Eyes, ears, skin, and nervous systems transform differences in light, pressure, temperature, and vibration into patterns that guide behavior. The brain may be understood, at least operationally, as an information-processing system produced by biological evolution rather than deliberate engineering.

This perspective does not yet tell us what information ultimately is. It tells us where to look: at preparation, transmission, decoding, use, and stabilization.

3. Paper, rulers, and the production of precision

Euclidean geometry did not emerge in a vacuum. It depended on practical means of producing and preserving forms: surfaces, straightedges, compasses, marks, and shared standards of acceptable construction.

A line drawn with a ruler is never a perfectly one-dimensional mathematical line. It has width, texture, irregularities, and finite resolution. If we magnify the paper and graphite far enough, their microscopic structure becomes visible. A pencil mark is not literally a point, and a drawn segment is not literally an ideal line.

But this does not make geometry meaningless. It shows that physical exactness and mathematical exactness are different achievements.

A geometrical practice can tolerate physical deviation while enforcing a stricter invariant. A line must be straight enough for the relevant operations and checks. A community can compare constructions, place a ruler against a mark, repeat a procedure, and decide whether the relation that matters has been preserved.

Working thesis. Mathematical precision is not the physical perfection of a mark. It is the reproducibility of selected distinctions and transformations despite irrelevant physical variation.

The scale of observation matters. A construction may count as exact relative to the resolution of its instruments and purposes. Mathematics then makes a remarkable further move: it separates the abstract relation from every particular instrument. Once the concepts of line, point, incidence, or continuity have been stabilized, they can be transported into contexts that the original drawing technologies never anticipated.

In this sense, idealization is a disciplined loss of information. Thickness, roughness, atomic structure, and manufacturing defects are suppressed so that incidence, order, distance, or continuity can be handled with greater logical clarity. The abstraction is useful not because it preserves everything, but because it preserves the relations needed for reasoning.

This raises a deep question: why are some abstractions so portable? Why can a concept first stabilized through macroscopic drawing practices later organize theories of microscopic matter, computation, or high-dimensional structure?

4. A concept is a reproducible capacity

A symbol on paper can remain physically stable for centuries. A mathematical concept in a brain is not stable in the same way. Neural activity is dynamic, distributed, context-sensitive, and constantly changing. The concept of a point is not a tiny static object stored at a fixed location in the head.

Stability in cognition should therefore not mean the persistence of an identical physical state. It may instead mean the reliable ability to re-enter a family of related activities.

When a trained mathematician encounters a familiar concept, certain inferential habits become available. The mathematician can recognize examples, perform standard operations, detect violations, connect the concept with nearby results, and reconstruct arguments. The exact neural state need not repeat. What repeats is a structured capacity.

Operational definition. For an individual intelligent system, a concept is stabilized when suitable cues reliably reactivate a sufficiently similar family of operations, expectations, distinctions, and inferences.

A concept may therefore be better understood as a reproducible way of navigating a space of possible reasoning, rather than as a single inner picture.

The word line illustrates this. An analyst may immediately activate limits, completeness, Cauchy sequences, order, and epsilon-delta arguments. A point-set topologist may foreground neighborhoods, connectedness, separation, or compactification. A homotopy theorist may use the interval as a parameter object for deformation. These are not unrelated concepts, but neither are they identical mental images. They are overlapping inferential repertoires shaped by different practices.

The point is not that every mathematician has a private, incommensurable meaning. With training, a person can move between these repertoires. Mathematical concepts are learnable patterns of operation, not immutable pictures permanently attached to individuals.

5. Several levels of stability

The word stability can refer to very different achievements. They should not be collapsed.

Level What is stabilized?
Physical persistence A mark, sound pattern, memory trace, file, or device state remains detectable or reconstructible for a relevant period.
Intrapersonal reactivation The same person can later re-enter a similar pattern of reasoning and use the concept consistently.
Pedagogical reproducibility An ordinary learner with suitable background can acquire the concept through a reasonable period of training.
Communal coordination Different members of a community can communicate, check, criticize, and extend reasoning involving the concept.
Cross-architecture interoperability Different kinds of intelligent systems, such as humans and AIs, can exchange mathematically useful inputs and outputs even when their internal primitives differ.

A private notation can be physically persistent and meaningful to one person without becoming a communal mathematical concept. Conversely, a communal concept can be instantiated by different neural processes in different people while remaining operationally shared.

A concept becomes public not because every brain contains an identical representation, but because training and communication align the actions that matter. Participants make compatible inferences, detect similar errors, and use the concept to cooperate.

Working thesis. Public mathematical stability is not identity of internal representation. It is reliable coordination of mathematical performance across persons, times, and media.

6. Education as a stabilization technology

The contrast between a textbook and a research paper makes this especially visible.

A textbook for beginners tries to make major inferential steps reconstructible by a reader who does not yet possess the field's mature intuition. A research paper assumes a different stabilized state. It omits transitions that an expert is expected to supply, activates familiar structures with compressed notation, and relies on a shared sense of what counts as routine.

This suggests an operational criterion for a human mathematical concept. The criterion should not be calibrated to rare geniuses, exceptional visualizers, or people who report receiving formulas in dreams. It should concern ordinary intelligent learners under reasonable professional training.

Operational definition. A mathematical concept is humanly stabilizable when an ordinary learner with appropriate prerequisites can, within a reasonable period of training, reliably recognize the concept, perform its basic operations, reconstruct its standard inferences, communicate about it, and transfer it to new examples.

This definition is deliberately graded. "Reasonable time" depends on the field, the learner's prerequisites, and the educational technologies available. But the criterion is testable in principle. We can ask how much instruction is required, how robust the acquired capability is, how far it transfers, and which media make learning more efficient.

Education is therefore not merely the transmission of completed mathematics. It is an information technology for producing reproducible cognitive capacities. Definitions, examples, exercises, diagrams, notation, proof styles, and mentorship all participate in stabilizing a concept inside new minds.

The most revealing sign of stabilization may be what no longer needs to be said. Once a pattern becomes fluent, intermediate steps can be reconstructed rather than explicitly supplied. Expert compression is possible because a community has invested in shared training.

7. Mathematics as a high-precision information practice

The broader project concerns understanding in physical systems, not mathematics alone. Animals, bacteria, nervous systems, scientific communities, and artificial agents all respond to their environments and may construct internal states that support prediction or control.

Mathematics is special within this larger picture because its norms of stability are unusually demanding.

This does not mean that every mathematical statement corresponds to a physically exact measurement, or that other sciences are imprecise. The claim is subtler: mathematical communities organize symbols, definitions, and transformations so that disagreements can often be localized and inferential steps can, in principle, be reproduced with exceptional exactness.

Mathematical accuracy includes at least five dimensions:

  1. Syntactic precision. Symbols and expressions can be distinguished and manipulated according to explicit rules.
  2. Inferential reproducibility. A proof can be checked by another trained person or by a formal system.
  3. Conceptual discipline. Definitions constrain which operations and analogies are legitimate.
  4. Composability. Stabilized results can be used as components in longer arguments without reopening every earlier step.
  5. Transportability. Abstract structures can be realized in different physical, scientific, and mathematical settings.

Mathematics may therefore be understood as a highly refined information practice: one that makes reasoning unusually portable, compressible, composable, and checkable.

A natural next question is whether this precision can be characterized quantitatively. Possible measures might involve robustness under re-encoding, error correction, proof reconstruction, transfer to new problems, or the amount of training required to reproduce an inference.

8. AI training and nonhuman concepts

The analogy between human education and artificial-intelligence training is incomplete but suggestive. Training can be long and expensive, while a trained system may produce a response in seconds. Humans also undergo years of education and later perform familiar inferences rapidly.

In both cases, a central question is how prolonged interaction with data and feedback reorganizes a system so that a family of responses becomes immediately available.

The deeper possibility is that an AI system may stabilize mathematical concepts that are not humanly stabilizable. After training on enormous bodies of mathematics, a system might develop internal structures that support reliable reasoning, compression, or discovery even though no human could acquire the same primitive structures through any reasonable curriculum.

Several claims must then be separated:

  • the AI may possess a stable internal capability;
  • humans may observe consistent inputs and outputs;
  • humans may verify proofs, predictions, or consequences;
  • yet humans may fail to acquire the AI's own primitive intuition.

Would that count as mathematical understanding? The answer depends on the operational criteria we choose. If understanding requires shared human intuition, then perhaps not. If it requires robust use, systematic generalization, error correction, and explanatory compression relative to the system's own architecture, then perhaps it does.

Working thesis. Conceptual stability is relative to a class of intelligent systems and to a training process. A concept may be stable for humans, stable for AIs, stable for both, or stable for neither.

This does not mean that every opaque model state deserves the name concept. Memorization or accidental correlation is insufficient. Stronger evidence would include systematic generalization, compositional use, counterfactual sensitivity, transfer between problems, and the ability to repair reasoning after errors.

9. Human intuition after AI discovery

Even when humans cannot internalize an AI's primitive concept, they may develop a second-order intuition around its behavior.

Scientists already work with systems that cannot be directly visualized. We use projections, analogies, invariants, effective theories, and observable signatures. A future mathematical community might interact with an AI-discovered structure through examples, queries, theorems, visualizations, compressed explanations, and formally verified consequences.

Over time, humans could stabilize a different concept that tracks the same regularities without reproducing the AI's internal representation. Human and machine concepts would then be connected through a translation interface rather than by identity. Their intuitions could differ while their mathematical actions remained coordinated.

This possibility changes the role of explanation. Explanation would no longer mean reproducing the discoverer's internal path. It would mean constructing a stable interface through which another kind of intelligence can test, navigate, criticize, and reuse the result.

10. The interface becomes part of mathematics

If two intelligences do not share primitive concepts, then the practical problem is to build an interface through which they can still do mathematics together.

A good mathematical interface would not merely display answers. It would expose enough structure for humans to interrogate and compose the system's results. It might provide:

  • human-readable conjectures and definitions;
  • formally checkable proofs;
  • examples and counterexamples selected for pedagogy;
  • multiple representations of the same structure;
  • uncertainty estimates and failure conditions;
  • interactive explanations adapted to the user's current conceptual repertoire;
  • tools for translating between human and machine-native abstractions.

The interface is not a superficial layer added after discovery. It may become the place where a new communal mathematics is formed. A machine-native concept becomes part of human science only when stable practices exist for interrogating, validating, criticizing, teaching, and composing its consequences.

Operational definition. Cross-system mathematical understanding is achieved when distinct kinds of intelligent systems can coordinate reliable mathematical action through an interface, even if they do not share the same internal representations.

The history of mathematics can then be read as a history of interfaces: speech, paper, diagrams, notation, textbooks, formal proof assistants, and now AI systems. Each medium changes which patterns of reasoning can be stabilized and shared.

11. Ten provisional theses

The discussion can be summarized in ten theses:

  1. Mathematics should initially be studied as an activity within civilization, not as a completed realm detached from history, bodies, media, or institutions.
  2. Information technology, in the broad sense, includes any physical means of preparing, preserving, detecting, and using distinguishable states.
  3. Spoken language, writing, diagrams, rulers, compasses, nervous systems, schools, and AI models belong to one extended technological ecology.
  4. Mathematical exactness is not literal physical perfection. It is the disciplined preservation of selected distinctions and transformations across variable physical realizations.
  5. An abstract concept in a mind is not a static neural object. It is a reproducible capacity to reactivate a structured pattern of reasoning.
  6. A communal mathematical concept is one that ordinary qualified learners can acquire in reasonable time and use in mutually checkable ways.
  7. Education is a technology for stabilizing such capacities across generations.
  8. Different kinds of intelligent systems may stabilize different primitive concepts.
  9. Human-AI collaboration may depend less on shared internal intuition than on reliable, inspectable, and improvable interfaces.
  10. Future mathematics may include machine-native structures for which humans develop only indirect or second-order intuitions.

12. Questions for the research program

This perspective leaves many questions open:

  1. What operational tests distinguish a genuine concept from memorized input-output behavior?
  2. How should "reasonable training time" be defined for humans and artificial systems?
  3. Can conceptual stability be measured through transfer, robustness, compression, compositionality, or error correction?
  4. Which external media made particular mathematical abstractions historically possible?
  5. When two systems use different internal primitives, what makes a translation interface faithful?
  6. Could an AI-generated concept be mathematically fruitful but permanently resistant to human stabilization?
  7. If humans build a second-order intuition around such a concept, in what sense are both systems understanding the same thing?
  8. How would proof, education, authorship, and explanation change in a mathematics no longer limited by human cognitive architecture?

Closing reflection

The discussion began with paper, pencils, rulers, and ancient geometry. It ended with the possibility of mathematical ideas native to artificial minds. The path between them was not accidental. Both ends concern the same problem: how one physical system can carry a pattern that another system can recover and use.

A line on paper, a definition in a textbook, a habit in a mathematician's mind, a proof shared by a community, and a representation inside an AI are not the same kind of object. But each can participate in a chain of stabilization.

Mathematics may be the civilizational practice that pushes this chain toward its highest currently known demands of precision, reproducibility, transportability, and composability.

The long-term question is therefore larger than whether AI can solve human mathematics. It is how mathematics changes when the systems capable of stabilizing concepts are no longer all human, and how different intelligences might build a common scientific world without sharing the same inner forms of thought.
